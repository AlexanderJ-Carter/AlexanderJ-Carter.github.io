Street photography is seductive: the city performs; you only need to be present. That is also why boundaries blur fastest — a "decisive moment" for the photographer can be an intrusion for the subject.

I have no legislatable doctrine, only constraints I review before leaving home. They make me shoot less; what remains I am more willing to publish under my name on alexander.xin.

## Read Light and Structure Before Aiming at Faces

If first instinct is always face close-up, ethical pressure peaks and technique gets lazy. I more often find:

- Light with character;
- A leading line or frame (doorway, glass, shadow);
- A stage to wait on (crossing, station exit, shop window).

When a person enters the stage, I photograph relationship to the city, not "stranger expression stock". Distance opens naturally; awkwardness and conflict drop.

## Distance Is Technique and Attitude

Focal length and foot position decide whether you enter private space. Long lens is not moral licence: distant candid of a smile can unsettle more than honest proximity.

I ask:

- If they turned and met my eyes, would I nod in acknowledgement?
- Out of context, could this image humiliate?
- Children, pain, embarrassment treated as amusement?

Any answer wobbles — put the camera down. The drive lacks one "almost interesting" frame.

## When I Speak Up

Recognisable face, possible exhibition or homepage use — I lean toward:

- Brief explanation after, ask permission;
- Or gesture before shooting;
- Refusal means delete, no "art requires it" argument.

Public-space law varies by region; **legal ≠ willing to publish**. Personal site under my name — my responsibility.

## Decisive Moment and Continuous Courtesy

Street myth says "never wait". Many strong images come from waiting — for light, for someone to enter composition, not lunging for faces. Waiting lowers aggressive presence and gives time to judge if the scene deserves the shutter.

Burst is fine; culling uses same severity as studio portraits: light, composition, moment; plus fourth — **respect.**

## Relation to Site Gallery

Before upload I re-check: does this place someone in unnecessary vulnerability for a stranger viewer? If yes — archive, crop beyond recognition, or do not use. Gallery is site character; character includes restraint.

## Publishing vs Personal Archive

Some frames stay on disk for my own memory but never go public — different bar. "Would I show this to the person in it?" is the publish test. "Did I learn something?" is enough for private keep.

## Gear and Visibility

Smaller camera sometimes helps; sometimes an obvious professional rig makes intent clearer. Neither removes the need to ask the four questions. Hiding lens does not hide ethics.

## When I Delete in the Field

If someone signals discomfort — wave, cover face, step away — I delete the last burst on camera before walking on. Arguing in the moment helps nobody. The image is not worth the story you would tell about "standing your ground".

## Teaching and Sharing Street Work

When I write about street photography on alexander.xin, I try to pair technique with restraint. Technique without ethics trains people to harvest strangers; ethics without technique produces preachy emptiness. Distance is where both meet.

## Crowds vs Individuals

Crowd scenes have different ethics than isolating one person in a moment of distress. I treat crowds as context; I treat singled-out vulnerability as high scrutiny. The same lens does not get the same moral pass in both cases.

## Local Norms and Travel

Travel street work tempts you to treat everywhere as "anonymous big city". Local norms may be stricter or looser than home. I read briefly, observe how other photographers behave, and default to more caution abroad — not because rules are harsher everywhere, but because I lack social context to judge harm.

## Closing Thoughts

Distance in street work is metres from subject and willingness to meet their gaze reciprocally. Look more, grab less, delete boldly, communicate when it matters — the city stays generous without trading offence for "someone in the frame".
