The camera is a box that records light. What makes a photograph work is the eye that frames it — what it keeps, cuts, and stresses. That is composition.

## Rules Exist to Be Learned

### Rule of thirds

Divide the frame into thirds; intersections anchor attention. The lesson is not "put subject on a dot" but **the subject need not sit dead centre** — balance begins there.

### Leading lines

Rails, rivers, roads — lines pull the eye toward a subject and beyond. Still images gain motion.

### Symmetry and centre

Symmetry declares completeness — architecture, reflections, corridors. Pure symmetry without texture or light variation can feel dead; one subtle break often revives it.

### Frame within frame

Doorways, windows, branches — depth and voyeuristic distance. You see someone who does not see you; narrative in the relationship.

## Beyond Rules

### Negative space

In Chinese painting, blank space is as composed as ink. Hiroshi Sugimoto's seascapes — half sky, half sea, thin horizon — show how emptiness can carry tension.

### Breaking thirds

Centre a portrait when you want confrontation — eyes lock with the viewer. Robert Capa broke rules because truth outranked geometry in chaos.

### Dutch angle

Tilted horizon signals unease when intentional; accidental tilt reads as mistake. Intent is the difference.

## From Photos to UI

Same problem: **organise visual information on a plane**.

- Hierarchy: subject/background ↔ primary/secondary UI.
- Contrast: light-dark, size, weight — restrained, not shouty.
- Whitespace: rhythm in layout like negative space in frame.

## Seeing Before Shooting

Learning photography changed how I walk streets — light angle, shadow length, wet pavement reflections. I shoot less now than when I started, because **seeing matters more than shutter count**.

Composition taught me: **what you exclude matters as much as what you include** — in photos, IA, and daily priorities.

You cannot put everything in one frame, one page, or one day. Choose. Cut. Let light into what remains.
